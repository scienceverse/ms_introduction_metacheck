Abstract

Meta-scientific research has revealed that researchers often fall short of implementing best research practices when designing studies, reporting findings, citing the literature, and sharing data and code. Two key barriers may exolain this: time constraints, which limit researchers’ ability to master and implement all aspects of the research process, and cognitive constraints, which make it difficult to consistently remember and apply all relevant practices in their work. By capturing expert knowledge in an automated tool, we aim to improve the quality of scientific research and promote a wider adoption of best research practices. We introduce Metacheck, an automated tool designed to detect (the absence of) best practices. We illustrate its capabilities by performing automated checks on 1937 open-access articles published in Psychological Science between 2014 and 2026. Researchers can use the tool to assess scientific articles during peer review or to check their own manuscript before submission. Metacheck is module-based and open source, allowing the research communit to extend its functionalities. Automated tools such as Metacheck have the potential to increase the adoption of best practices, support researcher education, and ultimately improve the quality of scientific reporting.

Keywords: Peer Review, Quality Control, Automation, Open Data


Part 1: Introducing Metacheck as an Automated Tool for Improving Research Practices in Psychology

Introduction

Meta-scientific research has identified substantial room for improvement in how researchers design studies, report findings, and implement open science practices when sharing their work. The adoption of best research practices has been slow (Sharpe, 2013) and often suboptimal. For example, researchers often use invalid and unreliable measures (Goos et al., 2024), misinterpret non-significant results (Aczel et al., 2018; Murphy et al., 2025), write insufficiently specific preregistrations (Akker et al., 2024), conduct invalid power analyses (Thibault et al., 2024), and misuse Bayes factors (Tendeiro et al., 2024). Although it is the responsibility of researchers to keep up to date with best research practices, time constraints and the rapid pace of new developments make this challenging. Peer reviewers, similarly pressed for time, may not identify instances where best research practices are not followed. Checklists have the potential to increase the adoption of best practices, but in practice, they have limited impact, as researchers might not be aware of the existence of checklists and they still need sufficient expertise to evaluate whether they have adequately adhered to each item (Dexter & Shafer, 2017).

Human factors research suggests that automation (where researchers remain in the loop) can offer a partial solution. Two recent developments have increased the possibility that best research practices can be automatically detected. First, meta-scientific research has identified where the adoption of best practices is lagging, and offers concrete suggestions for improvement. This work often includes data that can be used as ground truth to validate automated checks. Second, advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence have made it easier to deal with written text, both when turning scientific articles into structured data files (e.g., using the GROBID machine learning library, GROBID (2008--2025)) and extract or classify information in written text. This has made it possible to develop algorithms that emulate what experts do when they assess whether best research practices are present or absent. For example, a reviewer might search for the section in the paper that reports an a priori power analysis and then check whether authors have followed best reporting practices. Specifically, the reviewer would check whether authors report the alpha level, desired power, the effect size metric, a justification for the effect size, and the relevant statistical test(s) for each hypothesis (Lakens, 2022). If any of these components is missing, authors should be made aware that they are not following best research practices, so that the transparency of the power analysis can be improved. While many automated checks for best research practices will require only simple text matching based on regular expressions, more complex text extraction and classification is possible using large language models (LLMs).

In this article, we introduce Metacheck, a new tool that uses text search, algorithmic code, and optional LLMs to extract information from scientific articles including manuscript drafts, submitted or published articles, or preregistration documents. Based on this information, Metacheck generates a report that provides suggestions to improve reporting and open science practices. Metacheck is inspired by quality control processes in software development. For example, R package developers run a series of automated checks before submitting their package to the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN). These checks aim to identify potential issues and inform developers about how to address them. Similarly, Metacheck screens scientific articles for potential issues and identifies areas for improvement using algorithms designed to emulate how experts evaluate research articles. Its goal is to help researchers incorporate best research practices into their work.

Metachek is modular, allowing dedicated modules to be developed for any research practice that the community wants to improve. By helping authors identify and address common problems before submission, Metacheck can reduce the burden on peer reviewers and allow them to focus on aspects of research that cannot be automatically detected.

Values

Metacheck has been developed based on a set of values agreed upon by the development team. These values guide the design and implementation of this software and its associated projects. They also help users to understand why certain features, although technically possible, will not be implemented in Metacheck. The first five values focus on the assurances that Metacheck provides for its users, while the last four concern its role within the broader research community.

Quality Control: The knowledge, data, code and software we create will be verified for accuracy, using publicly available methods and measures, such that users have enough context to interpret the results.

Transparency: We are committed to open source software and open access datasets for validation.

AI Optional: The use of LLMs will be restricted to classification of existing text, not evaluation of the quality of practice. The use of LLMs will always be opt-in and transparently declared. We will prioritise non-LLM functions where possible, and limit use to cases where it provides substantial benefits that cannot be realised with other methods, such as regular expressions.

No Automated Evaluation: While our software is designed to assist the evaluation of research by highlighting aspects where practice may be improved, we will never support automated quality assessments, rankings or scoring.

Data Privacy: We will be fully transparent about what data we have access to and how those data will be used, as well as what will be shared with external services. Wherever possible, we will prioritise functions that share minimal data and services that have robust data protection. We will meet all EU data privacy regulations.

Accessibility: We will provide our knowledge, data, code and software to all potential users with minimal barriers to access. We will prioritise access that is not unduly limited by wealth, disability or technical knowledge.

Community Engagement: Our work will reflect and respect the needs of a diverse range of research communities, and benefit their research practices. We are open to critique from the research community.

Sustainability: We are committed to the long-term viability of our work. We will actively protect and future-proof our work through technical maintenance, governance structures and strategic planning to make it a durable and relevant public good.

Fair Recognition and Reward: Contributions to knowledge, data, code, and software will be uniquely and persistently identified, and benefit a diverse range of contributors.

Using LLMs

These values are particularly important in guiding when and how modules use LLMs. By default, modules operate without relying on LLMs. Users can manually enable LLM functionality, but rule-based approaches are always attempted before falling back on LLM-based solutions. LLM models can be run locally using either Ollama (see Metacheck’s manual, https://www.scienceverse.org/metacheck_book/chapters/ollama.html) or through a locally hosted model on a high performance computer (HPC) or graphics processing unit (GPU) cluster via vLLM .

Users can also disable modules that communicate with external servers, such as those that check references against external databases. Additionally, modules can check linked data and code files stored locally rather than retrieving them from online data repositories. Consequently, most checks can be performed locally, without sending manuscript content to external services. This allows users to run Metacheck while keeping their manuscript and associated research files on their own computer.

Use Cases

The current version of Metacheck is designed for two use cases. The first is to support researchers, either as authors or reviewers, who have the goal of improving a scientific manuscript by checking for the presence or absence of best research practices. Metacheck can provide recommendations for improving a manuscript in situations where: 1) a practice is not sufficiently salient to the researcher to be noticed without assistance; 2) the researcher lacks the knowledge or expertise needed to identify the absence of a practice independently; or 3) automatically checking for a practice saves substantial time or effort.

Researchers using Metacheck should be aware that recommendations generated by its modules can include both false positive and false negative errors. A false positive error occurs when a module flags a practice as potentially problematic when it is not, whereas a false negative error occurs when a module fails to identify an actual problem. Since there is an inherent trade-off between false positives and negatives, most of our modules follow a smoke detector principle: they prioritise detecting potential problems that a user might want to examine in more detail, even at the cost of generating false alarms. Consequently, modules may flag practices that turn out not to be problematic after inspection, while aiming to minimise the number of problems they miss. This approach is similar to that of spelling correction tools, which sometimes incorrectly flags words but remain useful because they also identify genuine mistakes.

As developers, our priority is to develop modules with sufficiently error rates to ensure that Metacheck remains useful to researchers. Following the ISO 9241-11 definition, usability refers to “the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use”. Our working assumption is that preparing a scientific article takes a considerable amount of time and that researchers care about the quality of their work. Under these assumptions, we believe that a tool with a relatively high false discovery rate (e.g., a 50% probability that a flagged practice is not actually problematic) can still be because it also identifies genuine problems in articles. For example, a spelling checker that flags both ‘Metacheck’ and ‘manuscritp’ as misspelled words but fails to flag ‘extend’ when the intended word is ‘extent’. The spelling checker has made two errors out of three checks, yet it remains useful as long as it correctly identifies an actual spelling mistake (‘manuscritp’). Similarly, just like users can add ‘Metacheck’ to their spelling dictionary to prevent it from being flagged, researchers can create customized versions of modules to avoid false positives for practices that differ across research fields.

The second use case for Metacheck is as a meta-scientific tool to examine large numbers of research articles. For example, a science funder might be interested in determining how many articles by funded researchers share data and code in accordance with its policies. A meta-researcher might want to examine how frequently effect sizes are reported in a specific literature and whether reporting practices have improved over time. A scientific journal might want to evaluate how a change in editorial practices has impacted the diversity in populations examined in the studies it publishes.

Metacheck modules can easily be run on large numbers of articles, facilitating text searches and the automatic extraction and classification of information. However, unlike the individual manuscript use case, meta-scientific applications require sufficently low error rates for users to rely on the results. Here, false negative errors are particularly problematic becayse they are difficult to detect without manually screening articles that were not flagged by the module. If manual screening of all articles were feasible, there would be little reason to rely on automated tools in first place. False positive errors might be easier to identify but still require users to manually check all instances flagged by a module.

Consequently, low error rates are essential for the usability of Metacheck as a meta-scientific tool. Validation procedures that quantify these error rates are therefore necessary to establish the accuracy of modules, help users determine whether a module is sufficiently accurate for its intended purpose, and allow researchers to report the corresponding error rates when presenting their findings. It is possible that a module has low enough error rates for helping individual researchers to improve their manuscripts, while still being insufficiently accurate for metascientifc purposes. To support informed decisions about their use, Metacheck reports validation information for each module in the report it produces.

Examples

In the following section, we demonstrate how to use selected Metacheck modules from both a researcher’s perspective and a meta-scientific perspective. We will illustrate the potential of automated checks to improve best research practices in four areas: 1) reporting guidelines experimental; 2) power analysis; 3) open science practices ; and 4) references.

We perform these checks on 1937 articles published in Psychological Science from 2014 to 2026, as this journal contains studies from all subfields in psychology, and is considered a high-quality outlet. If Metacheck can improve research practical in Psychological Science, the same will likely be true in for other outlets. All examples were produced using R (R Core Team, 2025) and the following R packages: dplyr (Wickham, François, et al., 2026), ggplot2 (Wickham, Chang, et al., 2026), flextable (Gohel & Skintzos, 2026), metacheck (DeBruine et al., 2026), readr and stringr (Wickham, 2025). This article was written using Quarto (Allaire & Dervieux, 2025).

Checking Reported P values

The Journal Article Reporting Standards (JARS) developed by the American Psychological Association (APA) offer guidance on the information that should be reported in scientific articles to enhance their scientific rigour (Appelbaum et al., 2018). Almost every published scientific article could be improved if researchers diligently followed JARS. However, the guidelines are not widely known, rarely implemented by authors, and almost never checked by editors or reviewers. Manually checking whether all reporting standards are implemented would be effortful. Automation can help by automatically assessing whether recommendations are followed.

The first reporting guideline that has been implemented in Metacheck concerns the reporting of exact p values. Access to precisely reported p values allows readers to include p values in meta-analytic tools, such as p-curve (Simonsohn et al., 2014) or z-curve (Schimmack & Brunner, 2017), and makes it possible to check the internal coherence of the reported results with tools such as Statcheck (Nuijten et al., 2016). The APA Manual (Association, 2020) states:

“Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001.”

Metacheck has a dedicated rule-based module, stat_p_exact, which returns all instances of imprecisely reported p values in scientific articles. It also warns for p values reported as p = .000, which are exactly reported, but incorrect, as a p value can never be 0, and which should have been reported as p < .001. For illustrative purposes, we run the module on a single paper and display, in Table 1, the p value that was reported with insufficient precision and the full sentence in which it was reported.

The modules also provide a recommendation for improving the research practice associated with the warnings they detect. For example, the stat_p_exact module returns the following recommendation:

“Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.”

All modules can be run in batch mode on a large number of articles, and the output can be used for meta-scientific research. One example is shown in Figure 1, which displays the proportion of imprecisely reported p values by statistical significance over time. The figure shows that the proportion of imprecisely reported p values is higher among significant than among non-significant results. Another example is shown in Figure 2, which displays the proportion of articles containing at least one imprecisely reported p values over time. The figure shows that there has been a decline in comparison to a decade ago.

The module has also flagged 19 p values of .000 across 11 articles, which could have easily been prevented had authors used Metacheck. Another example is illustrated in Figure 3, which shows the distribution of imprecisely reported p values by comparator symbol. This type of overview can reveal interesting exploratory patterns, such as a large peak around p > 0.25. This may indicate that researchers commonly use 0.25 as a threshold for interpreting a result as non-significant, although the origin of this practice is unclear. One possible source is the recommendation to use an alpha level between 0.25 and 0.35 as a stopping criterion in forward step-wise selection procedures (Bendel & Afifi, 1977), which was later popularized as alpha \le 0.25 in a widely used textbook on logistic regression (Hosmer Jr et al., 2013). However, we have no evidence this is the source of the convention. The correct approach for concluding that an effect is not meaningful (i.e., equivalent to zero) is to perform an equivalence test against a smallest effect size of interest (Lakens et al., 2018).

The performance of the stat_p_exact module was evaluated on 226 open-access articles published in Psychological Science, for which a ground-truth dataset was manually created. This set of articles contained 548 instances of non-exact p values and 4703 instances of exact p values. It correctly detected 433 of 548 instances (79%) and incorrectly flagged 70 of the 4703 precisely reported p values (1.5%). The module missed 115 instances (21%) of imprecisely reported p values, and correctly identified 4633 instances of precisely reported p values.

We have also developed a related module, stat_p_nonsig that detects whether non-significant p values are reported in an article. When such results are identified, the module warns researchers against the common practice of interpreting non-significant p values as evidence of the absence of an effect and returns all sentences containing non-significant p values, allowing researchers to quickly check for potential misinterpretations. Table 2 displays the non-significant p value and the full sentence in which it was reported.

When a non-significant p value is detected, the module provides the following recommendation on how to interpret the a non-significant result:

“Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05. It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.”

The module does not attempt to classify whether these statements containing non-significant sentences were misinterpreted, but authors interested in automating this step can use the llm function in Metacheck to send the results to a (local) LLM and retrieve the inference.

The performance of the stat_p_nonsig module was evaluated on 226 open-access articles published in Psychological Science, for which a ground-truth dataset was manually created. The set contained 5251 p values, of which 1401 were non-significant. The module correctly identified 1278 of the 1401 non-significant p values (91%) and missed 123. It correctly left 3703 of the 3850 significant p values unflagged (96%) and incorrectly classified 147 significant p values as non-significant. This corresonds top a false positive rate of 4%.

Checking (Un)reported Effect Sizes

A second JARS guideline that can be checked automatically is whether effect sizes are reported alongside their statistical test result. Each statistical test (e.g., t-test or F-test) should be accompanied by the corresponding effect size (e.g., Cohen’s d or \eta_p^2). Metacheck includes a dedicated rule-based module, stat_effect_size, that identifies t-tests and F-tests for which no effect size is reported and flags these instances for further inspection.

Figure 4 shows the results of applying the module to 1937 published articles in Psychological Science between 2014 and 2026. In 2014, 51% of t-tests and 37% of F-tests were reported without an accompanying effect size. By 2024, these proportions had declined to 13% and 16%, respectively, suggesting an improvement in reporting practices, although the proportion for t-tests was higher again in 2025 (42%). Tools such as Metacheck may help reduce this percentage even further by identifying missing effect sizes before articles are submitted or published.

The performance of the module was evaluated on 250 open-access articles published in Psychological Science, of which 161 reported at least one t-test or F-test. The module’s extraction of these 1469 tests was manually checked to create a ground-truth dataset. The module correctly identified 71% of t-tests reported with effect sizes and 26% of t-tests reported without effect sizes. For F-tests, the corresponding percentages were 81% and 11%. This level of accuracy is sufficient for meta-scientific research, such as examining changes in adherence to reporting guidelines over time.

Another feature of the stat_effect_size is that it checks whether reported effect sizes are consistent with the corresponding test statistic and degrees of freedom. For t-tests, it checks Cohen’s d and Hedges’ g, while for F-tests, it checks \eta_p^2 and \omega_p^2. Importantly, reporting an effect size such as d = 0.3 does not provide an information about the study design (i.e., whether the comparison is between or within subjects). The module therefore computes d under several plausible assumptions, including a Cohen’s paired d_z, independent with equal sample sizes, and a range of possible sample sizes for independent samples. If any of the recomputed effect sizes matches the reported effect size, the result is deemed as consistent. Table 3 displays some of the information returned by the module when checking the consistency of effect sizes in a single article. The first example shows a reported effect size that does not match the value implied by the t-test under any of the tested assumptions, whereas the second is consistent with an independent-samples t-test assuming equal sample sizes.

Checking Power Analyses

To design an informative study, researchers should collect a sufficient number of observations. The collected sample size should be justified with respect to the study’s inferential goal (Lakens, 2022). One approach to do this is by performing an a priori power analysis. When such an analysis is performed, it should be reported transparently so that peers can evaluate it.

A transparent power analysis report includes relevant details about the statistical test for which it was conducted (e.g., an independent t-test, a within-between interaction in a 2 x 2 ANOVA), the effect size magnitude and metric (e.g., Cohen’s d = 0.3, r = 0.5, odds ratio = 3.0), the power (e.g., 95%), sample size, and critical alpha (e.g., 0.05). The report should also be clear about which of these values was computed, which is often done using labels like “a priori”, “sensitivity” or “post hoc” (e.g., an a priori analysis computes sample size, while a sensitivity analysis computes effect size). The report should also justify the input values (e.g., a smallest effect size of interest). In practice, however, many articles omit one or more of these elements, making it difficult to evaluate the power analysis. In addition, some researchers perform post hoc power analyses based on the observed effect size and collected sample size, even though this practice is widely recognized as uninformative and should be avoided (Hoenig & Heisey, 2001).

The power module is designed to help researchers report power analyses more completely while discouraging the use of post-hoc power analyses. It can be used with or without LLM’s. Without LLM’s the module relies on regular expressions to identify paragraphs that contain the standalone word power (or powers/powered), at least one term associated with power analyses (e.g., effect size, sample size, a priori, sensitivity, or post hoc), and at least one numeric value, excluding four-digit numbers that are likely to represent publication years. Because this approach relies on regular expressions, only power analyses that explicitly contain terms such as “a priori”, “sensitivity” or “post hoc” can be classified. This use of the module is limited to the classification of power analyses and does not extract the reported components (e.g., effect size metric, statistical test).

A limitation of currently reported power analyses is that they lack a common reporting standard (e.g., similar to how the APA manual standardized the reporting of statistical tests). This lack of standardization makes it difficult, if not impossible, to reliably extract relevant information from sentences describing a power analysis using a rule-based approach alone. However, this limitation can be partially addressed by using an LLM to classify and extract information from reported power analyses. Based on a predefined prompt, the LLM returns a structured output containing the identified power analysis components.

For the 1937 articles published in Psychological Science, the module using the LLM-based approach currently identifies and extracts information from 663 articles containing 1295 identified power analyses. Not all missing information is equally problematic. For example, a missing alpha level is less concerning because the conventional value of 5% is used in most studies, although some researchers use more stringent thresholds (e.g., 0.01) or adjust alpha levels to account for multiple comparisons. By contrast, missing information about the statistical test is more concerning given the multiplicity of statistical tests used in psychology articles. Missing the expected effect size is also particularly problematic because this component is central to justifying the planned sample size.

Given the lack of standardization in how power analyses are reported and the complexity of human language, false positive and negative errors should be expected. For an example, the sentence “Null of 33% power Note: The observed p-curve includes 29 statistically significant (p < .05) results, of which 14 are p < .025. There were 8 additional results entered but excluded from p-curve because they were p > .05.” was incorrectly classified as a sentence describing a power analysis (i.e., a false positive) due to the presence of a significance level threshold, and numbers of entities included and excluded in the analysis.

The following sentence is an example of a false negative error: “We attempted to recruit 200 participants to have sufficient power to detect a small to moderate effect size, but in recruiting groups of friends on campus, we ended up with 199 fluent English speakers (105 female)”. This statement provides too little information about the details of the power analysis to be classified as one.

Checking Statements

So far, we have focused on modules that capture expertise about statistical practices. However, another category of checks does not require any specific expertise but can still benefit from automation. Although these checks are relatively straightforward, they can easily be overlooked by researchers and reviewers. Metacheck includes several modules that check for general statements commonly required by journals. For example, ethics_check searchers for statements indicating that ethical approval was obtained for research involving participants; funding_check searchers for funding statements; and coi_check searches for conflict-of-interest statements. Below we illustrate two additional modules, open_practices and prereg_check, which automate checks related to open science practices and preregistration, respectively.

The open_practices module searches for statements indicating that data, code, materials, or preregistration are openly available. It returns the sentences in which such statements are identified and classifies, for each type of open practice, whether a corresponding statement was detected. Table 6 shows the results of applying the module to a single article. The first statement indicates that both data and code are available, whereas the second indicates the availability of code only. The third statement reports that data, analysis code, and experimental scripts are publicly available. Therefore, the three statements have been correctly identified.

The module also flags statements indicating that data or materials are available only upon request. For example, in the sample of 1937 articles, the module identified 64 such statements. When an upon-request statement is detected, the module returns the following warning:

Some materials are shared on request, which is not an acceptable method of sharing. This is based on the following text:

Data for the present experiments are available on request from the corresponding author.

Another category of checks that does not require specialised expertise concerns preregistration. Preregistrations often go unchecked, which is problematic because studies may deviate from their preregistered plans without reporting these deviations. Metacheck includes the prereg_check module, which identifies links to preregistrations on OSF and AsPredicted in a manuscript, retrieves their text, and organises the contents into a standardised template. This makes it easier for reviewers to compare the preregistered plans with what is reported in the manuscript and identify potential discrepancies.

To illustrate its use, we show the output of prereg_check after running it on a single paper. When the module identifies links to preregistrations, it returns a summary indicating how many were found.

We found 3 preregistrations.

The module returns a table with one row per preregistrations. The columns correspond to fields in the standardised preregistration template. For example, for an OSF preregistration, the table includes fields such as hypotheses, sample size and statistical tests, among others.

We can then use the Metacheck’s text_search() function to extract relevant sentences from the manuscript and compare them with the corresponding information in the preregistration. For example, we can search for sentences containing the word “hypothesis” and compare the reported hypotheses with those specified in the preregistration to identify potential discrepancies. Below we compare the hypothesis specified in the third preregistration identified above with the hypothesis reported in the study.

Preregistration hypothesis

Study hypothesis

(H1) Adolescents in the motive-alignment intervention will exhibit more sustainable behavior compared to adolescents in the traditional environmental intervention. Sustainable behavior will be measured in terms of their (a) food choices at the post-intervention assessment, (b) food choices at the follow-up assessment, and (c) donations at the follow-up assessment.

Using Bayesian informative hypothesis testing, we evaluated the hypothesis that the motive-alignment intervention would foster more proenvironmental behavior than the control intervention.

The same approach can be applied to any preregistration field by searching the manuscript for relevant terms, such as those related to sample size or statistical tests. However, this approach requires users to manually compare the information reported in the manuscript with the corresponding preregistered information. To facilitate this process, we have also developed a Metacheck module that sends detected preregistrations to RegCheck, a fully automated comparison tool that uses LLMs to compare a manuscript with its preregistration (Cummins et al., 2026).

Checking References

Metacheck includes several modules that use an article’s references list of an article to perform automated checks. For example, ref_retraction searches for cited articles that have corrections, expressions of concern, or retractions; ref_replication identifies cases in which an original article is cited but a direct replication study of that article exists and is not cited, using the Replication Database (Röseler et al., 2024); ref_accuracy identifies inconsistencies in references; and ref_pubpeer checks whether cited articles have comments on PubPeer.

For illustrative purposes, we demonstrate the ref_pubpeer module, which checks whether references with a DOI have comments on PubPeer, a platform for post-publication peer review. These comments may be worth investigating, as they can range from minor clarifications to serious concerns about the work. The goal is to make this information more readily available to researchers. Note that a lot of comments on PubPeer are posted test results from an older version of the Statcheck R package. We filter these PubPeer comments out because they are generally uninformative. If the module detects any other comments on a cited article, it returns the following warning:

“We checked 9 references with DOIs. You cited 2 references with comments in PubPeer. Pubpeer is a platform for post-publication peer review. We have filtered out Pubpeer comments by Statcheck.

The module reports which cited articles have comments on PubPeer, the number of comments, and a link to the corresponding discussion, allowing users to assess the relevance of each discussion themselves. As illustrated in Table 7, two cited articles have 13 and 2 PubPeer comments respectively, with links to the corresponding discussions.

For reference-related modules to work reliably, references must be accurately extracted from the PDF file, and each reference needs to have a DOI. However, some journals do not require DOIs in their references, and older publications may not have one. These limitations make it more difficult to automate reference-related checks. Metacheck provides two options to retrieve missing DOIs from CrossRef or OpenAlex. One option is to set the crossref_lookup = TRUE argument in convert() function, which uses GROBID to convert a PDF file into XML or JSON format. Alternatively, users can apply add_bib_match() directly to a converted manuscript to retrieve DOI matches for its references.

Validation

Evaluating the accuracy of automated tools is essential for establishing their performance. The increasing availability of large language models (LLMs) has facilitated the development of automated tools, sometimes with limited direct human involvement in their design and testing. This makes systematic validation particularly important: How accurately do these tools perform, and under which circumstances can their outputs be trusted?

Quality control is one of Metacheck’s core values and reflects our commitment to evaluating the accuracy of its modules through validation. Ideally, validation should be conducted using datasets that are representative of the target population of articles to which an automated tool will be applied. This involves establishing ground truth and comparing it with the output generated by the automated tool. This comparison allows instances to be classified as true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, from which performance metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value (precision) can be calculated. Because these metrics capture different aspects of performance, it is important to explicitly report which metrics are used and how they are calculated.

Equally important is the transparency and reproducibility of the validation procedure. Documentation should describe how the ground truth was established, how human judgments were obtained, what constituted a correct or incorrect extraction, and which data were included in the evaluation. The limitations of the validation approach should also be clearly communicated, as these determine the conclusions that can be drawn about a tool’s performance.

For example, the validation of Metacheck’s stat_effect_size module involved using the module to identify instances of t-tests and F-tests and extract their corresponding effect sizes. The resulting validation dataset contained the sentences flagged by the module as reporting these statistical tests, together with the extracted effect sizes. Human coders subsequently reviewed the detected instances and classified the module’s outputs according to whether an effect size had been correctly extracted.

An important limitation of this validation approach is that the validation dataset was constructed using the module itself to identify statistical tests. Consequently, statistical tests that the module failed to detect were not included in the dataset and could not be identified as false negatives. The validation results therefore reflect the module’s accuracy in extracting effect sizes from detected statistical tests, rather than its overall performance in identifying all statistical tests and their corresponding effect sizes in an article. This distinction is important when interpreting the reported performance metrics.

We are committed to continuously strengthening our validation procedures, improving the documentation and transparency of our evaluations, and ensuring that our modules are assessed using reproducible workflows. As Metacheck evolves, we will continue to review and improve our validation practices to increase confidence in the accuracy of its modules.

Creating Metacheck Modules

Metacheck is modular by design. Its modules are conceptually similar to functions in R packages: each module performs a specific automated check, and provides feedback in a structured output format that can be further analysed or integrated in the reports that can be generated with Metacheck. This architecture reflects two core values of Metacheck: community engagement and epistemic diversity. We belive that decisions about what constitutes best research practice should emerge from scientific discussions rather than be imposed by a single group. Accordingly, Metacheck is designed to make it as easy as possible for researchers to translate their own views on best practices into modules.

Anyone can develop a module and validate its performance. Modules can be developed and installed locally or contributed to the Metacheck R package through a GitHub pull request. This flexibility allows journals to develop modules that verify compliance with their submission guidelines, methodological experts to automate checks for issues they frequently encounter during peer review, and organizations to create modules that assess adherence to their published reporting guidelines.

Through its modular architecture, Metacheck can also serve as a hub for existing automated tools. An increasing number number of tool developers are creating useful automated checks. By providing a single entry point to all available automated checks, Metacheck makes them easier to access and discover while providing a common infrastructure for PDF import, file conversion, text searching, and structured output.

As proof of concept, we developed a Metacheck module for Statcheck (Nuijten et al., 2016). However, consistent with Metacheck’s emphasis on validated automated checks, the module only implements check for t-tests and F-tests. An initial validation study indicated that for t-tests, F-tests, and \chi^2 tests, Statcheck achieved a true positive rate of 85.3%, increasing to 100% when one-tailed tests were explicitly identified using terms such as “one-tailed”, “one-sided”, or “directional” (Nuijten et al., 2017). By contrast, Statcheck has not been validated for correlations (r) or Z-tests, and anecdotal evidence suggests substantially lower accuracy for these tests. Accordingly, the Metacheck statcheck module excludes correlations and Z-tests by default until its performance on these tests has been validated.

Discussion

We have demonstrated the functionality of some of the modules in a newly developed automated tool, Metacheck, on articles published in Psychological Science. Through manually created ground truth, we have validated its performance, and illustrated the ability of Metacheck to detect instances where published articles do not follow best reporting practices. Our results of the modules show that automated tools can identify opportunities to improve articles that have passed peer review, including articles that may have undergone additional checks by Statistics, Transparency, and Rigor (STAR) editors. When the number of best practices exceeds what editors and researchers can easily remember, and it is not feasible for every editor and researcher to become an experts in all of them, capturing this expertise in automated tools can increase the quality of scientific articles.

Metacheck is under continuous development. In separated articles, we will describe additional modules that examine data and code practices and assess the reporting of power analyses. The data and code modules can identify the absence of best practices in code (e.g., hard-coded file paths and setting working directories), potential issues in datasets (e.g., personal information and manual data-entry errors), issues in data repositories (e.g., missing meta-data or codebook, files requires to run the code), and even automatically check the computational reproducibility of R code. The power analysis module identifies and classifies power analyses and detects key information that is missing, helping researchers improve the reporting of their power analyses. Together with the modules presented here, these developments aim to provide a comprehensive set of automated checks for quantitative scientific articles in the social sciences.

The current Metacheck modules capture our own expertise in quantitative research, particularly in methods and statistics, transparency, and open science practices. This makes the tool immediately useful to researchers conducting quantitative research and sharing data and code. However, the modularity of Metacheck allows researchers with expertise in other areas to develop modules that capture additional best practices. As the number of modules grow, the infrastructure provided by Metacheck should also make it easier to develop new modules and extend the number of research practices that can be checked automatically.

Authors can use Metacheck to improve their articles before submission. If using Metacheck contributes to improvements, authors can cite it, just as they would cite any other R package used in their research. It is important for users to realize that Metacheck is designed to assist the evaluation of research, not to automate it. Users should critically evaluate the output of its modules because automated checks can produce both false positives and false negatives. A flagged issue may not require improvement, while an unflagged article may still contain problems. Furthermore, many important aspects of articles cannot currently be evaluated through automation, including the clarity of conceptual definitions, the coherence of theoretical predictions, the appropriateness of study designs and statistical analyses, and whether conclusions follow from the data and analysis. By identifying aspects of articles that can be checked automatically, Metacheck can assists researchers in evaluating scientific articles, but the final judgement must remain with human experts.

Although it may seem desirable to only release modules that achieve very high accuracy, our current development strategy follows the Pareto principle: we prioritize to identify common issues for which automated checks can deliver the greatest improvements. Reducing the error rate of automated checks from 50% to 20% is generally easier than reducing it from 20% to 5%. Furthermore, we believe that improvements in automated checks will eventually not only depend on refining algorithms accurately, but also on how clearly researchers communicate their methods and results. Vague or ambiguous statements can be lead to false positives and false negatives. Rather than continually adapting the algorithms to accomodate human prose, researchers could help reduce errors rates by communicating their research more clearly and explicitly.

The mathematician Alfred Whitehead remarked: “Civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which can be performed without thinking about them. Operations of thought are like cavalry charges in a battle - they are strictly limited in number, they require fresh horses, and must only be made at decisive moments” (Whitehead, 1911, p. 61). Metacheck is built on the conviction that scientists should use their cognitive resources to important intellectual challenges rather than manually working through lengthy reporting checklist, checking whether cited articles have received post-publication peer review comments, or remembering every disclosure required by a journal. We believe that reporting standards are important, but that they should require as little effort as possible, allowing scientists to focus their time and energy on challenges that demand their full attention. By automatically generating reports that identify whether, where, and how reporting and open science practices can be improved, Metacheck aims to make evaluation and improvement of scientific articles as straightforward as possible.
